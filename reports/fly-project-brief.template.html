From wiring to a living loop.

Give a virtual fly a taste sensor and one gut-feedback mechanism. Test whether its dietary history changes how the same brain circuit responds to sugar.

Explore the proposed experiment

Research proposal Literature reviewed; simulation not yet implemented.

Evidence reviewed 25 Sep 2026 · Prepared 26 Sep 2026

The question is simple: can the same circuit react differently to the same food because the fly’s body state has changed?

A focused starting pointOne sensory modality. One physiological mechanism. One intervention we can compare with real experiments. The full report preserves our research and the review of all 96 linked repositories.

What we actually have.

The release gives us measured wiring. Our project would add a specific model of how physiology changes the activity flowing through it.

Measured anatomyPublished experimentsOur proposed model

Google and its collaborators announced MaleCNS on September 3, 2026: a wiring map of an adult male fly’s brain and ventral nerve cord. Data were available before the announcement. It is an anatomical reconstruction; runnable simulations supply additional assumptions about neuronal dynamics.[1]

A connectome tells us which cells connect. It does not specify every receptor, effective synaptic strength, hormone response, or metabolic process. Those are part of the modeling work.

Existing resources already supply parts of an embodied animal: spiking models, physical bodies, visual-system models, and data tools. Their interfaces still need scientific justification.[2][7][8]

Our aim is to add and validate one physiological feedback loop. A complete cloned fly remains a much broader aspiration.

The anatomy is real

MaleCNS contains roughly 166,700 reconstructed neurons. Different releases and filters yield different counts.[1]

The dynamics are modeled

The Shiu model has task-specific experimental validation for feeding and grooming. That evidence does not automatically transfer to our proposed extension.[2]

The new loop needs testing

We must identify target cells, calibrate the input and readout, and test predictions against measurements held out from fitting.

Same sugar.
Different history.

Start with taste and one gut-derived signal. Compare a standard diet with a high-sugar diet, then block the gut signal and test the response again.

Published work in adult male flies links dietary sugar to gut-derived Hedgehog signaling and reduced sweet sensitivity. Gut-specific suppression removes the usual diet-dependent reduction in proboscis extension.[3]

Match the timescale.

This concerns dietary history over hours and days, with early-adult timing effects. It is not an instantaneous satiety switch. The intervention is gut-specific; local Hedgehog signaling in sensory neurons has a different role.[3]

Our first version can stay small.

Present sugar to a stationary virtual fly. Measure sensory activity and a proboscis-extension readout. Replay diet-dependent physiological states first; add dynamic ingestion and gut updates after the basic circuit response is validated.

A model should be able to be wrong.

Programming a suppressive feedback and observing suppression demonstrates the rule we wrote. Scientific value comes from predicting data that were not used to fit the model.

Calibrate sensory input

Constrain the concentration-to-activity mapping using suitable physiological measurements.

Freeze circuit + readout

Use the same dynamics and action mapping across conditions. No per-condition retuning.

Predict held-out responses

Test unfit concentrations or interventions against the corresponding biological measurements.

Compare simple alternatives

Check fixed physiology and a model without the connectome. Test whether wiring adds value.

What we would measure

  • Activity in the selected taste and motor neurons.
  • Probability or latency of proboscis extension.
  • Changes under the gut-specific intervention.
  • Sensitivity to uncertain model parameters.

What the behavior means

Proboscis extension is a feeding-initiation response. It does not establish swallowing, digestion, or energy storage. Those require their own models and measurements.[4]

Physiological state can change a response while synaptic weights remain fixed. That is not automatically learning.

One fly first.
Interactions next.

The immediate goal is a controlled biological assay. A social model becomes useful once we can trust the individual’s sensory and physiological responses.

  1. Establish the circuit

    First technical gate

    Choose MaleCNS or FlyWire explicitly. Match the sensory and feeding cells, pin the data, and validate a baseline sugar response.

  2. Add one body signal

    Initial prototype

    Compare two diet histories with intact and suppressed gut feedback. Hold the readout fixed. Display model and experimental results with their uncertainty.

  3. Close the feedback loop

    After input–output validation

    Add an explicit intake model and time-dependent gut state. Test slow dynamics against appropriate temporal data.

  4. Introduce social experience

    Later research stage

    Model relevant sensory interactions and a social-to-physiological pathway. Compare isolated and grouped flies using defined behavioral endpoints.

A fly-specific bridge to social experiments

Chronic isolation has been shown to increase feeding and reduce sleep in flies despite uninterrupted food access, with particular peptidergic neurons implicated. That offers a relevant follow-up benchmark. The gut model alone would not generate this mechanism automatically.[5]

Calhoun’s famous Universe 25 case involved mice in a confined population. It does not establish that abundance alone causes depression. Reproduction or population collapse in flies would require additional social, reproductive, and developmental models.[9]

Details for the build discussion.

The proposal is feasible to investigate because we can separate its components. These are the decisions to resolve before treating the prototype as a biological model.

Dataset, cell identity, and sex

MaleCNS, female FlyWire, and female BANC are different reconstructions. Their neuron identifiers are not interchangeable. The 2022 feeding-circuit experiments used female flies; the proposed gut-feedback benchmark used males. Verify cell matches and the applicability of physiological findings.[3][4]

The new gustatory-connectome work maps sensory inputs into feeding pathways and supplies a useful anatomical starting point.[11]

We should not describe simulator-provided food distance as natural taste. Likewise, a global “hunger” multiplier is not a substitute for identifying where a signal acts.

Endocrine feedback is not a universal reward signal

A circulating gut signal and local neuromodulation can both change behavior through different mechanisms. Food-deprivation-dependent dopamine signaling in sugar-sensing neurons offers a separate, more acute benchmark.[12]

Choose one mechanism first. Do not merge every hormone into a scalar or call every change in neural activity learning. Extra components add parameters and validation work.

Compute and population scaling

Connectivity should be stored sparsely. In our illustrative calculation, a 166,700-cell dense float32 matrix occupies about 111 GB; a minimal CSR graph with approximately 25.6 million directed edges occupies about 205 MB. These are array-storage estimates, excluding neural states, delays, copies, and learning overhead.

Multiple flies can share immutable graph data while retaining separate neural and physiological states. A thousand runs sample the model; they do not remove shared model error or become a thousand independent biological specimens. Actual throughput still needs benchmarking.

What we have not established
  • No simulation or biological benchmark was executed in this session.
  • The benchmark’s numeric data have not been extracted or fitted.
  • The cell mapping, hormonal kinetics, and behavioral readout remain to be validated.
  • We have not established a connectome advantage over simpler models.
  • The review is not an exhaustive novelty search across neuroscience.
  • A full fly clone, subjective depression, and reproductive collapse are not demonstrated outcomes.
